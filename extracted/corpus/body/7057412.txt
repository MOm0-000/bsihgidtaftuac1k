Among many statements which can be readily accepted at the time are some which have an increasingly puzzling quality. Comments such as 'Someone has got out of bed the wrong side this morning', 'It's a woman's privilege to change her mind' and 'Thus conscience doth make cowards of us all' are concerned with moodiness, and each statement leads to a further question of how or why. 

Arising from the discussion of the semantics of emotion by Owens & Maxmen (1979), emotion is regarded for present purposes as a transient response assisting the individuaf to overcome obstacles to the path of adaptation, whereas 'mood' is the setting of sensitivity of the emotional response, the threshold, which may be low or high, alert or sluggish. Moodiness is rapid variation in this threshold and loss of the homeostatic mechanism which, in most people, holds mood at a steady position characteristic for that individual. As moodiness is no new development, innumerable literary references might be expected. Morphological guides to character afforded full scope for identifying this trait in the lines of the palm, the contours of the head or the face. Surprisingly, there is little reference to it in such classical works as those by Lavater (1850) and Gall & Spurzheim (1809), but earlier beliefs such as those of astrologers attributed moodiness to those born under Gemini, being ruled by the sign Mercury. The twins symbolizing this constellation are said to pull in opposite directions, as one or other dominates the contest, so that variation occurs. This is perhaps a symbol for a servo system or homeostasis, and its occurrence in fields of heavenly study are particularly relevant to the research to be described. The absence of awareness of homeostasis in relation to mood from writings of later centuries can be explained by the gap in continuing development of philosophy in the Dark Ages and the gradual rediscovery, culminating in the exposition of this general concept by Claude Bernard (1949). 

Factors in moodiness Man is probably unaware at the time of most of the factors which influence mood. Inevitably an individual attributes a mood change only to current circumstances as he perceives them. The factors usually apparent to those affected are: 

Diurnal moodiness. The common tendency among healthy individuals to feel more cheerful either in the afternoon or evening or alternatively in the mornings (Elsass et al. 1979). Moodiness as part ofbeing emotionally upset, as by some obstruction to the path of adaptation. This is likely to be most severe after loss of a love object. Marked sensitivity in personality. The hysterical personality is associated with sudden mood changes, fickleness and manipulative behaviour (Slavney et al. 1977, Rabins & Slavniey 1979, Slavney & Rich 1980). Types ofpsychopathy: Moody psychopaths are readily recognized. It is assumed that they do not develop or develop imperfectly those sentiments which govern conduct in group or social situations. Control of mood level To appreciate how these factors could produce moodiness, it is necessary to consider the mechanism on which mood depends. Recognition and identification of words, colours, scenes and organized consciousness depend on appropriate sensory collation and referral to the memory store. From an extremely rapid review of past experience we gain an awareness of the emotional response required and we experience an emotional feeling tone. The memories reviewed are not simple factual records. Shand (1896), considering memory from the purely psychological aspect, described as sentiments the organization of emotional dispositionsin systems about the various classes and objects which unite them. This concept can now be interpreted in terms of neurophysiological hypothesis from the excellent review by Eccles (1973). 

If the conscious thinking process is represented by a reverberating electrical circuit in the central nervous system, memory storage can occur only if action is taken and if an emotional input is provided. These events take place in the limbic system, the visceral brain, using the Papez circuit. The emotional label for the otherwise factual memory is provided by the amygdala and is duly attached by the hippocampus to allow storage of the memory to take place in the temporal lobe. In this way current experience becomes learned fact, and the neurophysiological equivalent of this factual record, with its accompanying emotional feeling tone, is referred to as an engram. 
Classification of sentiments or engrams 

To be available for the rapid review required in emergency situations, engrams must be classified in a functional sense. This is strikingly demonstrated by Young's (1962) work with octopus in which the recording of memory associated with outgoing behaviour, aggressive and feeding activity takes place in a part of the central nervous system separate from the storage of withdrawal, solitary and inhibiting behaviour modes. Although it is not envisaged that an anatomical dichotomy exists in man, there is evidence of a functional separation which maintains its integrity in the healthy person throughout life.

It is interesting that the network envisaged, established by the visceral brain, could be the basis to which people now refer when commenting on their 'gut reaction'. 

It is assumed that in everyday life the individual uses this framework of memory as a basis for appropriate behaviour, viewing each situation in the light of instinctual need and indications, both logical and emotional, from his own memory store, before deciding an appropriate response. If the individual succeeds in satisfying instinctual needs while gaining approval, he experiences a feeling of gratification and satisfaction. If he transgresses the accepted code of his culture, he experiences guilt. 

While taking decisions, a helpful factor is the tendency to view past memories selectively, remembering easily past, rewarded, experiences, and excluding painful, unrewarded or dismal memories. Using a theatrical analogy, the pleasurable memories are warmly coloured and brilliantly lit, while painful ones are only poorly illuminated, appearing in cold tones. The concept of the hysterical personality 

If an individual's memory store consisted largely of emotional labels with very little factual content, as in Figure 3, the structure would function much as the hysterical personality appears to behave. 'Thinking' is limited to the emotional field. There is no logical network to enable reality to be tested or to hold the ambition steady. Such individuals are likely to be unduly influenced through emotional excitement and are potentially gullible. There is a greater risk of irrational ideation and activity. Perception is inclined to be intuitive. Conflicts are more likely to be resolved by emotional manipulation and behaviour is more likely to include emotional display.

There is no emotional network on which ambition can be based or from which motivation can be derived. It is as if the engrams are haphazardly recorded (Figure 4). In the event of a serious loss, the expression of grief will allow recovery, but as Freud (1925) and Lindemann (1944) observed, failure to accept the loss emotionally can lead to the development of depressive illness. A comparable response in animals is despair. Porsolt and his colleagues demonstrated that this state, induced in drowning mice, can be delayed by the administration of antidepressants (1978a) or by electroconvulsive applications (1978b). Hrdina & von Kulmiz (1977) have shown that separation-induced reactions in infrahuman primates can be lessened by administration of an antidepressant. Lishman (1972), using the semantic differential test, showed an alteration in hedonic selectivity in memory associated with depressed affect. Sorting tests applied by Smolen (1978) to depressives indicate that they tend to underestimate their actual performance, while Teasdale & Fogarty (1979) found that the time to retrieve pleasant memories was significantly longer when patients were depressed. 

This change of view has its counterpart in group animal behaviour in the massive self destructive exodus of the arctic lemming and the springbok (Ricard 1971). Similar changes in the way of looking at the past as part of depressive illness can be induced by many common physical and biochemical agents. These include high pyrexial illnesses, virus infections, cerebral lesions, myxoedema and almost all endocrine conditions, cortisone, some oral contraceptive compounds and especially potent analgesics and barbiturates. It has been suggested that certain malignancies can modify behaviour and mood by changing endorphin metabolism (British Medical Journal 1980). 
Moodiness of acute schizophrenia 

Signs of the process occurring in acute schizophrenia can be understood using the same model. It is necessary to assume a changed relationship between the factual and emotional parts of engrams forming part of the previously healthy person's memory store. If perhaps, by a change in amine balance, the emotional tags became detached from their previous factual labels and were free to link with inappropriate factual memories, the sudden unpredictable mood changes, lack of rapport, the knight's move in thought and incongruity of affect, which characterize the acute illness, could be explained (Figure 7). It is accepted that intelligence is preserved, but that drive, ambition and emotional warmth, all dependent on the network of emotional parts of engrams, are progressively lost in untreated patients. If the process continues to the so-called 'burnt out' state, mood apparently has no setting, and the patient is unable to relate to others or to show motivation.

The central view 

The evidence available suggests strongly that the neurophysiological basis for this mechanism of differential viewing of past memories is hypothalamic. Not only is the evidence for selfstimulation of the hypothalamus in rats (Olds & Olds 1963) and in goats (Persson 1962) impressive, but the reports of Foerster & Gagel (1933) and Grinker (1934) suggest that a similar function is carried out in the hypothalamus in man. Turning from moodiness which could arise (a) from a conflict between an individual's engrams and reality, (b) from a faulty development of engrams, or (c) from chemical changes affecting the memory store itself, there is another group which appears to depend on disturbances of those mechanisms which allow certain metabolic and behavioural responses to remain in phase with rhythmic environmental changes. The best-known disturbances of this linked phasing are unsatisfactory shift-working conditions, jet-lag, slimming by severe dieting or continued lack of sleep. These, with the common diurnal mood variation are, with premenstrual endocrine changes, associated with hypothalamic function. 

Related to much longer cycles are those rare but classical examples of seasonal moodiness seen with great clarity in some patients whose depressive episodes develop insidiously in autumn and lift dramatically in spring. In these cases the change in sleep pattern is towards deeper, long sleep with day-time lethargy and the illness is resistant during its seasonal period to the treatment methods which will resolve the majority of solitary illnesses. These illnesses have both a seasonal similarity and clinical likeness to hibernation (Lange 1928, Pollitt 1960). As the central organ of hibernation is the hypothalamus, it would be reasonable to assume a disturbance in its function in man in depressive mood states. Suicide levels which reflect moodiness have been shown by Swinscow (1951) to be highest from spring to about mid-summer in England and Wales, with a similar seasonal distribution in Australia. A seasonal trend in incidence for the admissions of female manic patients, with a peak in August and September, has been shown (Symonds & Williams 1976). Takahashi (1964), using Japanese information, confirmed other links between mood, temperature and central homeostatic functions. He found a seasonal increase in suicide, but also an increase in conception over the same months, when the temperature is usually above 20°C. This evidence may indicate an increase in drive in late summer, the resultant behaviour depending on which

To appreciate the full extent of the vulnerability of the nervous system to factors capable of influencing the mood regulatory mechanism, it is necessary to see the hypothalamus serving four main areas of adjustment. Those of which we are most aware are the functions of adjusting the internal environment and keeping an appropriate supply of substances to the organs of the body. The second function is the one under discussion as a basis for moodiness, in that the hypothalamus reflects the level of emotional response through the autonomic nervous system, thereby forming the chief mechanism of adjustment to the external environment. The third area of regulation is of reproduction. A fourth and particularly important function assisting adaptation is its capability of allowing the organism's metabolic and alerting mechanisms to be linked in phase with climatic, celestial and terraqueous rhythms to ensure survival. Common experience suggests, and a teleological approach demands, that all these functions affect each other. If they all monitor their own fields, it is conceivable that this manifold regulator, while assessing the feedback from body metabolism, from the immediate emotional situation and from the many celestial and terraqueous rhythms, can be upset by a disturbance in any of these three areas. Mood setting, being part of this mechanism, would be affected as part of the resultant response. 

Among the phenomena which may relate to external rhythms are observations suggesting that monthly metabolic (and possibly mood) rhythms are detectable in men (Luce 1971), and although the influence of the full moon in inducing lunacy has not been convincingly demonstrated, a correlation between moon phases and admissions for psychosis was found by Climent & Plutchik (1977). Lieber & Sherin (1972) found a positive association between homicide and the new moon in two widely-separated areas in the United States, and Davenhill & Johnson (1979) showed a firm relationship between personality test scores and moon phases. In the search for a pathway of possible lunar influence in man, investigations in lower animals have shown a positive lead. The effect of lunar cycles on circadian rhythms in constant light and temperature has been demonstrated in hamsters (Brown 1965), cockroaches (Harker 1958), fidler, sand and green crabs (Webb & Brown 1958, Naylor 1963, Chandrashekaran 1965). Of particular interest is a 180 phase shift in lunar cycle obtained with water planarians by a 180- experimental shift in the ambient horizontal magnetic vector, suggesting a magnetic field factor (Brown & Park 1965). Although this research involved primitive creatures, the mechanism in man under review is the most primitive regulating system, well represented in fish, birds and beetles. Possibly the apparatus in man is but a blunted version of a much more sensitive system of greater use in animals having fewer ways of immediate adjustment. Whether blunted or not, it is conceivable that certain skills such as dowsing are related to perception of magnetic forces affecting the earth. Tromp (1949) believed dowsing to be a purely physiological phenomenon; and Harvalik & de Boer (1976), using metal shielding, claimed to have identified two receptor areas for dowsing sensations: the adrenals and a situation between the pituitary and the pineal. An example of what was believed to be a similar sensitivity in animals was described by Castaret who caught bats in the Tignahustes Caves and took them in light-tight boxes by train. As the train passed their cave all the bats showed signs of nervous activity and set up desperate squeaks (Ricard 1971). In relation to climatic changes, Tromp (1949) noted clusters of manifold increase in suicide in Holland, where the prevalence is relatively low. The only association to be found was in sudden changes in weather. This point was also raised by Skutch (1973) ina letter to the British Medical Journal. 

Hot winds have a widespread effect and cause a greater incidence of migraine, irritability, accident proneness, depression, loss of self control, diminished reaction time, increased suicide attempts and respiratory disturbance.

The effect of the hot winds can be linked with effects of positively-charged air ions. Two aspects are relevant here. Krueger & Reed (1976) demonstrated that positive air ions tended to release serotonin into tissue while negative ions reduced the effect. Professor Felix Sulman and his colleagues (1970) studied 800 khamsin sufferers over four years, finding a raised blood serotonin level which he could reproduce experimentally by positive ions and restore by exposing the patients to a negative ion atmosphere. Assael et al. (1974) showed that positive ions can reduce the frequency of the alpha rhythm and increase its amplitude up to 20%. This field is still embryonic but the evidence available links environmental electrical effects with metabolic changes relevant to mood regulation. 

Much more research has been conducted to explain the action of sun spots on human mood. Sun spots, which go unnoticed at a conscious level, appear profoundly to influence behaviour and particularly mood (Luce 1971). It appears that the electromagnetic fields produced by these flares are sufficient to affect body receptor sites. During a total eclipse of the sun, which cuts off all the solar radiation to the earth over the whole electromagnetic range, the phases of all animal biological rhythms are shifted, and their biological clocks deranged (Wojtusiak & Majlert 1976). The eclipse causes secondary effects on the electrical state of the atmosphere, the magnetic field of the earth, temperature, pressure and humidity, which may be relevant. 

Piccardi (1962) worked with inorganic colloids and activated water produced by using mercury and a neon tube in water. Over a period of eight years he measured the rate of precipitation and observed the number of sun spots occurring each day. In two experiments he used the colloid suspended in activated water and in ordinary water, one pair being shielded with metal, the other exposed. He repeated the test using ordinary water in both tubes. He found a high correlation between the different precipitation rates of the colloid and the sun spot number. 

A few of many reports on the effects of both electromagnetic fields and solar radiation on man are relevant. Links between both the heart rate of healthy individuals and the growth rate of children with fluctuations in the earth's magnetic field have been demonstrated (Playfair & Hill 1978), while Poumailloux & Viart (1959) found a marked rise in the incidence of cardiac infarcts at times of solar activity peaks.

If these celestial factors are relevant, the common claims to detect an imminent weather change by feelings in the bones, stiffness in joints or pain in an injured limb can be understood in scientific terms. The subjective feelings experienced before thunderstorms and the awe with which earlier races viewed the eclipse appear justified. Perhaps these galactic influences constitute Jung's (1916) 'vital force' mediating the universal effects of the 'collective unconscious'. 

Perhaps the strictest test of the theory and model is its suitability for application to therapy and its ability to explain defences against moodiness. One defence is the maintenance of high morale, and if morale can be seen in terms of the way in which past classified engrams are illuminated, high morale would be the setting of mood to include the conviction of success of high personal and group worth and devoid of the possibility of failure. The commonly adopted methods of maintaining high morale by emphasizing the positive aspects of the person, the group, the aim, the past and future is clear. Negative suggestions are vigorously avoided. These influence the way of viewing past engrams, but other factors relating to the substrate of mood and particularly the hypothalamus are also well known. The adverse effects of loss of sleep and hunger on morale have assumed importance in major battles. During the more civilized battles of the 13th and 14th centuries, before gun-fire altered the approach, the classical mode of improving morale was by good food and conditions (influencing the hypothalamic substrate), drumming up (rhythmic effect), bugling, trumpeting, bagpipes, shouting and cheering (emotional effect), thereby satisfying all the conditions required to avoid moodiness and the risk of demoralization. Excitement evoked by these sounds is accompanied by piloerection, a direct hypothalamic reflex. Nothing is new, and although William Shakespeare made Hamlet say, 'Thus conscience doth make cowards of us all', he must have appreciated that with an appropriate mood setting, and the necessary positive thinking to increase morale, heroes emerge. Evidently he wished to indicate Hamlet's depressed state, viewing only the gloomy aspects at that moment! Nevertheless, he showed accurate knowledge of the mechanism in his treatment of moodiness in 'The Taming of the Shrew'. First Petruchio blows hot, then cold, stimulating first the positive then the negative toned engrams; a well known brain-washing technique (Sargant 1957). Clearly the shrew's defences were good, but when he began to interfere with the shrew's homeostatic mechanisms and circadian rhythms, she turned into a harmonious, dutiful wife! Recent research by Baker (1980a, b) has shown that geomagnetic factors may influence human orientation in the horizontal plane. This is reminiscent of the findings described by Ricard (1971). Confined birds exposed to the night sky for the first time for a matter of minutes were able to orientate themselves accurately, suggesting a continuously sensory physiological clock and an electromagnetic factor. Perhaps electromagnetic fields from earth and sky can affect both mood and orientation. If so, reference to the wrong side of the bed may be more realistic than humorous.
