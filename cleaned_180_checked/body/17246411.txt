COMPARISON of genetic structure between species can provide information about the relative 
importance of various evolutionary forces, i. e., migra- 
tion, natural selection and genetic drift, that shape 
the pattern of genetic variation within species, and 
about the genetic mechanism or “strategies” of adap- 
tation that are employed to deal with the variation in 
the physical and biotic environments between species. 
Comparisons of genetic structure are usually done 
between closely related species (e.g., see HUBBY and 
THROCKMORTON 
1968; AYALA and POWELL 1972; 
LAKOVAARA, 
SAURA and FALK 1972; PRAKASH 1977; 
EISSES, VAN DIJK and VAN DELDEN 1979; GONZALEZ 
et al. 1982; OHNISHI, KAWANISHI and WATANABE 
1983), and if such species are similar in their geo- 
graphic and climatic distribution, so much the better 
as this reduces the number of variables to be examined 
and makes the comparison easy. 
The sibling species Drosophila melanogaster and Dro- 
sophila simulans are such as species pair. They are the 
two better known species of the ”melanogaster com- 
plex’’; both are cosmopolitan (BOCK 1980) and suff- 
ciently versatile to adapt to both temperate and trop- 
ical regions (PARSONS 1975, 1983). Their similar dis- 
tribution increases the probability of finding similarity 
in their genetic mechanisms of adaptation. A question 
of evolutionary significance is whether or not these 

sibling species have used the same genetic strategies 
in adapting to their varied environments. This prob- 
lem can be pursued by asking: Do these sibling species 
have similar patterns of genetic variation over their 
geographic and climatic distributions? 
The above question has been the focus of attention 
in a number of comparative studies on genetics, mor- 
phology, ecology and behaviour of this sibling pair 
(for reviews, see PARSONS 1975, 1983; PARSONS 
and 
STANLEY 1980). Recent studies have shown that the 
two species differ in the overall level of genetic vari- 
ation within populations, and geographic differentia- 
tion between populations for various genetic ele- 
ments. For example, polymorphic inversions occur in 
D. melanogaster (STALKER 1976; ASHBURNER 
and LE- 
MEUNIER 1976; METTLER, VOELKER 
and MUKAI 1977; 
KNIBB, OAKESHOTT 
and GIBSON 1981) but not in D. 
simulans (ASHBURNER 
and LEMEUNIER 
1976). D. si- 
mulans has been shown to be substantially less poly- 
morphic than D. melanogaster for allozymes (O’BRIEN 
and MACINTYRE 
1969; BERGER 1970; KOJIMA, GIL- 
LESPIE and TOBARI 
1970; TRIANTAPHYLLIDIS 
1973; 
STEINER, 
SUNG and PAIK 1976; TRIANTAPHYLLIDIS 
et 
al. 1980, 1982; CABRERA 
et al. 1982; HYYTIA et al. 
1985; WATADA, TOBARI 
and OHBA 1986; SINGH, 
CHOUDHARY 
and DAVID 1987), two-dimensional elec- 
trophoretic (2DE) proteins (OHNISHI et al. 1982) and

mitochondrial DNA (BABA-AISSA 
and SOLIGNAC 
1984; 
HALE and SINGH 1985). Taken together, these studies 
show that D. simulans has less of both the genetic 
variation within populations and genetic differentia- 
tion between populations than D. melanogaster. 
One reason for the differences in the genetic struc- 
ture of these species may be that relatively fewer 
populations and genes have been studied in D. simu- 
lans than in D. melanogaster. In fact, as the number of 
populations and genes studied in D. simulans has in- 
creased, the differences of heterozygosity between the 
two species have decreased. In view of the large inter- 
locus variance of heterozygosity (FUERST, CHAKRA- 
BORTY and NEI 1977), it is important to sample a large 
number of loci; and, preferably the same set of ho- 
mologous loci should be studied in both species. Here 
we report the results of a study of genic variation with 
114 gene-protein loci in four mainland (from Europe 
and Africa) and an island (Seychelle) populations of 
D. simulans. The results are compared with those 
obtained on the homologous set of protein loci in D. 
melanogaster (SINGH and RHOMBERG 1987b). The 
comparison shows that D. simulans is as heterozygous 
as D. melanogaster but the former shows significantly 
less geographic differentiation than the latter. Hy- 
potheses are discussed to explain the apparent contra- 
diction between the ecological similarity and genetic 
differences between the two species. 
MATERIALS AND METHODS 
The D. simulans lines used in this study came from five 
geographic locations-South 
France (55 isofemale lines), 
Tunisia (52), Congo (45), Capetown, South Africa (32) and 
Seychelle Island (26). The flies were maintained as isofemale 
lines on corn meal medium at 20". Unlike our previous 
study (SINGH, 
CHOUDHARY 
and DAVID 1987) in which we 
deliberately sampled loci that showed latitudinal clines in D. 
melanogaster or loci that were polymorphic in D. simulans, 
in the present study we sampled almost the entire set of 
allozyme loci that have recently been studied in D. melano- 
gaster (SINGH 
and RHOMBERC 198713). 
A total of 114 gene loci were studied, of which 79 code 
for enzymes and the remaining 35 for abundant proteins of 
unknown functions. Of the latter, 19 were stained by Coo- 
massie Brilliant-Blue, and the others by ultrasensitive silver 
stain (MERRIL, DUNAU and GOLDMAN 
198 1 ; MORRISSEY 
1982). 
The extraction and electrophoretic buffers used in this 
study were the same as described in SINGH, HICKEY 
and 
DAVID (1 982), SINCH 
and COULTHART 
(1 982) or SINCH and 
RHOMBERG (1 987b). Larvae were used for LAP, APH and 
larval proteins; pupae for EST-IO and GOT; and adults for 
all other enzymes and silver proteins. The samples were 
prepared by grinding ten larvae or adults in 50 on buffer. 
The crude extract was centrifuged at 16,OOOg for 5 min at 
4, and the supernatant was used for electrophoresis. Elec- 
trophoresis was carried out on vertical slab-gels following 
the standard methods (PRAKASH, 
LEWONTIN and HUBBY 
1969). [Sequential electrophoresis was not used as it has 
shown a relatively low level of hidden variation in D. melan- 
ogaster (CHOUDHARY 
and SINCH 1987).] For most of the 
enzymes, 5% gel of commercial grade acrylamide (Cyno- 
gum) and a continuous buffer system was used [see SINCH 
and RHOMBERC (1 987b) for details]. Run times for electro- 
phoresis varied from enzyme to enzyme and were adjusted 
for optimum separation. The staining methods were 
adapted from HARRIS 
and HOPKINSON 
(1 976), SMITH (1 976) 
or as referred in SINCH and RHOMBERC (1 987b). Isozyme 
loci and electrophoretic alleles were designated as described 
in SINCH, HICKEY 
and DAVID (1 982). 
RESULTS 
Of the 114 loci studied, 44 were found to be poly- 
morphic in D. simulans and 60 in D. melanogaster. 
Number of alleles, average heterozygosity and fixa- 
tion index for polymorphic loci in the two species are 
shown in Table 1. The data for D. melanogaster have 
been analyzed and reported elsewhere (SINGH and 
RHOMBERG 
1987a, b). Here we analyze the data for 
D. simulans and compare the results with those of D. 
melanogaster. 
Table 2 shows a summary of genic variation in five 
geographic populations of D. simulans. An average 
population is polymorphic for about 30% of its loci 
and an average individual is heterozygous at 9% of its 
genes. All five populations are highly heterozygous 
but the Continental tropical population (Congo) shows 
higher heterozygosity than the temperate populations. 
However this difference appears to be limited to only 
the enzyme loci; the abundant proteins have similar 
heterozygosity in all populations. The difference in 
heterozygosity arises from the fact that the Congo 
population contains a number of polymorphic loci 
(e.g., 6-Pgd, Hex-1, a-Gpd, Odh) that are monomorphic 
in the remaining populations. The polymorphic loci 
are about equally heterozygous in all populations. 
Similar differences in heterozygosity between temper- 
ate and tropical populations are reported in D. melan- 
ogaster (SINGH, HICKEY and DAVID 1982), but here 
the differences arise mostly from the highly poly- 
morphic loci and seem to disappear when the compar- 
ison is made with respect to all loci (SINGH and RHOM- 
BERG 1987b). The geographically isolated Seychelle 
population is less heterozygous than Congo although 
both are tropical in location. 
The distribution of polymorphic loci with respect 
to number of alleles and the frequency of the most 
common allele is shown in Figure 1. Two points 
emerge from these data. First, in both species the 
majority of polymorphic loci have only two to five 
alleles and very few loci have more than five alleles. 
Half of the polymorphic loci (48% in D. simulans and 
50% in D. melanogaster) have only two major alleles 
with a frequency of 10% or more, and fewer than 5 
loci in both species have three or more alleles with 
individual frequencies of 10% or more. Second, a 
larger proportion of di- and tri-allelic loci in D. simu- 
lans show an uneven distribution of allele frequencies, 
i.e., one allele is common and the others are rare. The 
multi-allelic loci, on the other hand, appear to be 
more evenly distributed in D. simulans than in D.

DISCUSSION 
D. melanogaster and D. simulans are two of the eight 
cosmopolitan species in the genus Drosophila (BOCK 
1980). The two species are morphologically almost 
identical except the posterior process of their male 
genital arches (STURTEVANT 
1920). Although widely 
spread, both species are restricted in occurrence in 
most parts of the world to urban or farm environ- 
ments or other places in which natural habitats have 
been modified by human, and therefore they are 
regarded as “domestic species” (PARSONS 
1975, 1983). 
Except in certain tropical regions where they occur in 
natural habitats, both species are unable to survive 
winters outdoors and are reintroduced annually from 
manmade habitats. Their demographic profiles are 
similar in sympatric populations but different in allo- 
patric populations, thus apparently showing a parallel 
response to environmental variations (DAVID et al. 
1984). With exception to alcohol, both species appear 
to utilize similar environmental resources (MCKENZIE 
and MCKECHNIE 
1979). 
In view of these similarities, one would expect to 
find similar the levels of genetic variation within pop- 
ulations and the genetic differentiation between pop- 
ulations of the two species. While similar latitudinal 
variations have been reported for some phenotypic 
and physiological characters (TANTAWY, 
MALLAH and 
TEWFIK 
1964; DAVID and BOCQUET 1975; PARSONS

1980) as well as for some allozymes (ANDERSON 
and 
OAKESHOTT 
1984), previous studies from our labora- 
tory as well as from those of others (HYYTIA et al. 
1985; WATADA, TOBARI 
and OHBA 1986; SINGH, 
CHOUDHARY 
and DAVID 1987) have shown that D. 
simulans differs from D. melanogaster in having less 
both genic heterozygosity within populations and geo- 
graphic differentiation between populations. The re- 
sults from the present study show that while both 
species have about the same level of mean heterozy- 
gosity and mean number of alleles, D. simulans still 
shows significantly less geographic differentiation 
than D. melanogaster. 
On the 99% criterion of polymorphism (i.e., the 
most common allele being 99% or less), D. melanogas- 
ter shows more loci polymorphic than D. simulans 
(52% vs. 39%: t = 1.989, P < 0.05). This is mostly 
due to the fact that D. melanogaster harbors low fre- 
quency alleles at many loci which are monomorphic 
in D. simulans. In fact, if we use a more stringent 
criterion of polymorphism (i.e., the most common 
allele being 95% or less), the number of polymorphic 
loci is about the same in the two species (39 in D. 
melanogaster and 34 in D. simulans). The presence of 
more middle repetitive and nomadic DNA (DOWSETT 
and YOUNG 1982) and of possibly “breakage inducing 
factors” (VOELKER 1974; YAMAGUCHI, 
CARDELLINO 
and MUKAI 1976) or mutator loci (GREEN 1976) in D. 
melanogaster are suggestive of higher mutation rate in 
this species and can potentially explain the high per- 
centage of polymorphic loci but is not obvious how 
these genomic mechanisms can have an effect on the 
allozyme variation per se. 
The high genetic differentiation between Seychelle 
and mainland D. simulans requires an explanation. 
The high heterozygosity and the presence of unique 
allozyme alleles, together, rule out the possibility that 
high genetic differentiation of Seychelle is due to 
recent founder effect. Recently it was shown that D. 
simulans from Madagascar island is also significantly 
differentiated from the mainland (HYYTIA et al. 
1985). These results suggest that D. simulans in Sey- 
chelle and Madagascar has been isolated from the 
mainland for a longer period of time and may even 
represent an ancestral race quite different from the 
one that gave rise to the mainland D. simulans. These 
speculations appear to be supported by the results 
from work on mitochondrial DNA (SOLIGNAC 
and 
MONNEROT 1986; L. R. HALE and R. S. SINGH, un- 
published data). D. simulans from Seychelle has a 
mitochondrial DNA variant which is quite different 
from that found in the mainland but very similar to 
the one found in Drosophila sechellia and Drosophila 
mauritiana. In view of the results on mitochondrial 
DNA, a comparison of allozyme variation among these 
related species would be interesting which we would 
take up elsewhere; here it should suffice to say that 
the high genetic differentiation between Seychelle and 
mainland D. simulans appears to be due to their long 
isolation, and thus in the following the discussion of 
genetic structure between D. simulans and D. melano- 
gaster would be limited to their continental popula- 
tions. 
The most obvious explanation for the differing 
amount of geographic differentiation between two 
species is gene flow. All other things being equal, 
species with less gene flow would show more geo- 
graphic differentiation. Gene flow should be an im- 
portant consideration in the present case as all genetic 
and phenotypic markers that have been studied show 
less geographic differentiation in D. simulans than D. 
melanogaster. Estimates of Nm based on F,, values (Fst 
= %Nm + 1 : WRIGHT 193 1) show that to explain the 
differences of geographic differentiation between D. 
melanogaster and D. simulans would require a two- to 
fourfold lower gene flow in the former than in the 
latter species. On the other hand, estimates of Nm 
based on the distribution of rare alleles (SLATKIN 
1985) are very similar in these species, ranging from 
1.27 to 3.09 in D. simulans and from 2.74 to 5.83 in 
D. melanogaster (SINGH and RHOMBERG 
1987a). Fur- 
thermore, D. melanogaster enters human habitat more 
readily than D. simulans (J. R. DAVID, personal com- 
munication) which would suggest that the level of 
gene flow in D. melanogaster, if anything, should be 
more not less than that in D. simulans. Thus, if any- 
thing, the differences in the level of gene flow in the 
two species are in the wrong direction and cannot 
explain the differences of geographic differentiation 
between the two species. In the following, we discuss 
four hypotheses which individually or in combination 
can explain the differences of geographic differentia- 
tion. A brief description of these hypotheses has been 
presented elsewhere (SINGH, CHOUDHARY 
and DAVID 
1987). 
Neutral-mutation hypothesis: Our expectation to 
find similar pattern of geographic variation in the two 
species was based on the implicit assumption that 
allozyme variation is representative of genes enabling 
these species to achieve their worldwide distribution 
and climatic adaptation. But the fact may be other- 
wise, i.e. allozyme variation may not be representative 
of the genetic variation which is the basis of similar 
adaptation in these species. In other words, allozymes 
may be irrelevant or neutral with respect to climatic 
adaptation. This hypothesis does not seem tenable as 
reduction in variability and/or geographic differentia- 
tion in D. simulans is also observed for chromosome 
inversion (ASHBURNER 
and LEMEUNIER 1976), mor- 
phological and quantitative variation (DAVID, BOC- 
QUET and DE SCHEEMAEKER-LOUIS 
1977; HYYTIA et 
al. 1985; WATADA, OHBA and TOBARI 
1986), middle- 
repetitive DNA variation (DOWSETT and YOUNG 
1982), 2DE proteins (OHNISHI et al. 1982) and mito- 
chondrial DNA restriction length polymorphism 
(BABA-AISSA and SOLIGNAC 1984; HALE and SINGH


1985). Since all genetic elements consistently show, 
on the average, less geographic differentiation in D. 
simulans than D. melanogaster, and since the levels of 
gene flow appear to be similar in the two species, the 
neutrality argument even if accepted for the majority 
of protein variation within the individual species can- 
not adequately explain the varying levels of geo- 
graphic differentiation between these species. 
Population bottleneck and recent colonization hy- 
pothesis: In most colonizing species genetic variation 
appears to be lower than those of comparable noncol- 
onizing species (PARSONS 
1983). In the past, a popu- 
lation bottleneck has been invoked to account for low 
level of variation in a diverse variety of organisms 
(HAIGH and MAYNARD SMITH 1972; SCHWAEGERLE 
and SCHAAL 1979; BRYANT, VAN DIJK and VAN DEL- 
DEN 198 1 ; NEI, MARUYAMA 
and CHAKRABORTY 
1975; 
MARUYAMA 
and FUERST 1985a,b; CHOUDHARY 
and 
SINGH 1987). That colonization has reduced the over- 
all amount of genetic variation is evident from the 
fact that in both species ancestral populations from 
tropical Africa show more genic diversity and more 
private alleles (LEMEUNIER 
et al. 1985; SINGH, HICKEY 
and DAVID 1982; HYYTIA et al. 1985; SINGH, CHOUD- 
HARY and DAVID 1987, and this study). But neither 
heterozygosity nor number of alleles per locus give 
any indication of a recent bottleneck in D. simulans. In 
this respect, the only relevant difference between the 
two species is in the proportion of polymorphic loci, 
which can be satisfactorily explained by assuming 
higher rate of mutation in D. melanogaster. However, 
if it could be shown that D. simulans has become 
cosmopolitan much more recently (evolutionarily 
speaking) without entailing a severe bottleneck, then 
the low geographic differentiation can be explained 
simply by arguing that D. simulans has had less time 
to equilibrate with its physical and biotic environment 
than D. melanogaster. 
Niche-width hypothesis: According to this hypoth- 
esis the amount of variation in a species is proportional 
to the niche-width of that species (VAN VALEN 1965; 
ROTHSTEIN 1973). It is argued that a species with a 
wide spectrum, say, in its food resource or environ- 
mental adaptation, is likely to have more variety of 
genotypes than one with a narrow spectrum of food 
resource or environmental adaptation (DA CUNHA, 
BURLA and DOBZHANSKY 
1950; DA CUNHA 
et al. 1959; 
POWELL 197 1 ; MCDONALD 
and AYALA 1974; POWELL 
and WISTRAND 1978; STEINER 1977; LACY 1982). 
Because of its ecological relevance and its ability to 
maintain genetic variation without genetic load, the 
niche-width model has a wide appeal among popula- 
tion biologists (e.g., SeeJOHNSON et al. 1969; HAMRICK 
and ALLARD 1972; TAYLOR 
and POWELL 1977; DE 
ALBUQUERQUE 
and NAPP 1981) although it is not 
without criticism (e.g., see WILSON 1969; SOULE and 
STEWART 1970; HALLETT 1980; ATKINSON and 
MILLER 1980). 
The relationship between genetic variation and 
niche-width has also been considered in D. melanogas- 
ter and D. simulans (BERGER 1971; JOHNSON and 
SCHAFFER 
1973; MINAWA and BIRLEY 1975; DE AL- 
BUQUERQUE and NAPP 1981; MACKAY 1981). D. me- 
lanogaster is reported to be physiologically and behav- 
iorally more flexible than D. simulans [see PARSONS 
(1 975, 1983) for review] but the niche-width variation 
that appears to be of major significance here is one of 
a difference in their range of climatic adaptation. 
Although both species are cosmopolitan and co-occur 
extensively, D. melanogaster is more successful than D. 
simulans in the temperate regions. D. melanogaster can 
tolerate wider temperature fluctuations than D. simu- 
lans (MCKENZIE 1974; MCKENZIE and PARSONS 
1974; 
PARSONS 
1979), and on a seasonal basis D. mezanogaster 
builds up large populations in the spring or early 
summer, and D. simulans in the late summer (PARSONS 
1975). Therefore, the difference in the levels of their 
geographic differentiation could be a result of their 
varying capacity for macroclimatic adaptation. 
Selection hypothesis: The low geographic differ- 
entiation in D. simulans may result from a selection 
“strategy” favoring broad adaptability of “a single 
purpose genotype” ( c j BAKER 1965). Under this hy- 
pothesis both species would be assumed to have similar 
niches but they differ in their genetic mechanisms of 
dealing with it. Species with similar niche-width may 
have qualitatively different genetic strategies (ANGUS 
1980; LYNCH 1983; PARKER et al. 1977), i.e., some 
may respond to the environmental gradient as fine 
grained while others react as coarse grained with 
resultant selection, respectively, for or against poly- 
morphism independent of niche width. For example, 
both D. melanogaster and D. simulans may respond to 
a temperature gradient, however, the former may 
strongly react to incremental changes and conse- 
quently show altered gene frequency but the latter 
may react less strongly with no significant alteration 
of gene frequency or may even respond to the same 
range of temperature as “acceptable” with no conse- 
quent polymorphism. The observation of fewer gene 
clines in D. simulans than in D. melanogaster provides 
evidence for this scenario. However, besides differ- 
ences in attraction to alcohol and light dependent 
dispersal (MCDONALD 
and PARSONS 1973; PARSONS 
1974), little is known about their natural environ- 
ment, which may not be all that different as the 
sympatric populations of these species appear to pos- 
sess an equal amount of genetic variation. Therefore, 
if the selection hypothesis is relevant in this case, the 
differences in their natural environments must be of 
spatial nature (e.g., geographic and/or climatic) which 
would explain why they have similar levels of hetero- 
zygosity within populations but different levels of 
differentiation between populations. An observation 
in favor of selection hypothesis and against recent 
and SCHULTZ 1979;JAENIKE, PARKER and SELANDER

bottleneck is that while all the three major chromo- 
somes, i.e., X, 2 and 3, are equally heterozygous in 
both species, only chromosomes X and 2 show signif- 
icant reduction in geographic differentiation of D. 
simulans (Table 3). 
The above hypotheses are not mutually exclusive, 
but they do differ in their relevance to account for 
the different features of genetic variation between the 
two species. In a preliminary report of this study 
(SINGH, CHOUDHARY 
and DAVID 1987) we concluded 
that bottleneck effect and niche-width appear to be 
the leading hypotheses to account for the differences 
in both the amount and the pattern of genetic varia- 
tion between these species. This conclusion was based 
on the observation that D. simulans has lower hetero- 
zygosity than D. melanogaster. The present results 
show that the major difference in the genetic variation 
of these species is not in the heterozygosity or number 
of alleles per locus but in the levels of genetic differ- 
entiation between populations. These conclusions are 
supported by our study of 2DE proteins in these 
species (COULTHART 
and SINGH 1987a,b). Thus, these 
results appear to rule out the role of population bot- 
tleneck but we are presently making another check of 
this hypothesis by looking at the mitochondrial DNA 
of these species. If the role of bottleneck can be 
satisfactorily ruled out, then we must conclude that 
variation in niche-widths and/or in genetic “strategies” 
of adaptation are the major contributing factors to 
the varying levels of geographic differentiation in 
these species. More work is required before we can 
choose between these two hypotheses.
